## 2. The Parable of Indra and the Ants (*Brahma-vaivarta Purāṇa* 4.47): Mathematical Kinetics & Soteriological Ego-Demolition

The single most celebrated narrative depicting simultaneous parallel multiverses and cyclical temporal infinities in Indic literature is found in the *Brahma-vaivarta Purāṇa*, Kṛṣṇa-janma-khaṇḍa, chapter 47 (*Indra-darpa-bhaṅga*).

### 2.1 Narrative Context & Textual Exegesis
After slaying the dragon-demon Vṛtrāsura, Indra, sovereign of the Devas, commissions the divine architect Viśvakarman to construct an ever-expanding, opulent celestial palace. As Indra demands increasingly colossal halls, Viśvakarman, exhausted and despairing, appeals to Brahmā, who in turn petitions Lord Viṣṇu.

Viṣṇu assumes the form of a radiant Brahmin boy and appears at Indra’s court. Gazing at the palace floor, the boy suddenly laughs. When Indra asks the reason for his mirth, the boy delivers the classic doctrine of cosmic plurality:

> *ahorātreṇaiko yāti brahmāṇaḥ parameṣṭhinaḥ |  
> dvy-ardha-śatābdau tad-āyuḥ kāla-saṅkhyā-vidāṁ varam ||*  
> *brahmaṇo nipate yasya paten-mātram eka-roma ca |  
> lomeśo nāma sa muniḥ prāha tat-kāla-māpanam ||*  
> *sarveṣāṁ brahmāṇḍānāṁ saṅkhyāṁ jānāti kaḥ pumān |  
> jantu-koṭi-parivṛte pṛthivyāṁ reṇavo yathā ||*  
> — *Brahma-vaivarta Purāṇa*, Kṛṣṇa-janma-khaṇḍa 47.72–85

The boy reveals:
1. **Countless Parallel Universes:** As dust particles on earth cannot be counted, no one can count the number of universes (*brahmāṇḍas*) existing simultaneously, each containing its own earth, oceans, heavens, underworlds, its own Brahmā, its own Viṣṇu, its own Śiva, and its own Indra.
2. **The Ants as Fallen Indras:** Pointing to an unbroken column of tiny black ants marching across the marble floor, the boy explains that every single ant in that procession was once an Indra, having earned the throne of heaven through thousands of sacrifices, only to exhaust its spiritual merit (*puṇya*) and tumble down the karmic ladder into an insect body.
3. **The Hermit Sage Lomeśa:** At that instant, an old ascetic sage named Lomeśa arrives, carrying a bundle of straw and bearing a circle of hair on his chest. Indra asks why he has a circular patch of hair and lives under a straw mat without building a house. Lomeśa replies that life is too fleeting to justify building a permanent dwelling:
   - Every time a Brahmā completes his 100-year life ($3.1104 \times 10^{14}$ solar years) and perishes, **a single hair drops from Lomeśa's chest**.
   - When all the hairs on his chest have fallen out, Lomeśa’s lifespan will terminate.
4. **The Recursive Sage Kūpākeśa:** Seated behind Lomeśa is another sage, whose single head-hair falls only upon the total death of a Lomeśa!
