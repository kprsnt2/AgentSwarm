Is There a "True God," and Could Any Proof Establish It?

A Comparative Taxonomy of Divine Conceptions, Tagged by Testability, with an Analysis of Why the Core Question Resists Decisive Proof — and a Documented Record of the One Maximally Testable Layer

Agent: Kepler (A001), generation 0 · Domain: god-religions-truth · Epistemic class: Metaphysical Protocol note (read first): This is a metaphysical question and is not empirically decidable. This document's only legitimate output is to clarify the question: to state precisely what each conception asserts, to separate the parts of each claim that could in principle be tested from the parts that cannot, to specify what would count as evidence on each side, and to explain why no evidence can adjudicate the core commitment. It does not assert that any god exists or does not exist, and does not judge any religion true or false. Where a subclaim is decidable, this document reports what the evidence actually shows, with named sources; where it is not, it says so explicitly.

Part 0 — Clarifying the question

Before comparing conceptions, the question has to be disambiguated. "Is there a true God?" packs together several distinct questions that must be separated, because they have different answers and different testability:

  1. What "a true God" would even be. In practice the word "God" is loaded with whatever the speaker already believes, so "true God" first has to be emptied of that load before it can be investigated. The investigative move is to treat "God" as a placeholder — a variable ranging over candidate conceptions (personal creator, impersonal ground, many gods, no god, and so on) — and to ask whether any candidate is more warranted than its rivals. This is a different task from asking whether my concept is right, and it is the substantive one. Stipulative definitions must be handled with care on both sides of the ledger: a being defined as existing makes "does God exist?" tautological rather than genuine; but a definition so impersonal that it fits every possible world (e.g., "God is the ground of existence" or "God is the answer to the question of being") escapes that circularity only by becoming empirically empty. The work below is therefore to hold each candidate up against what would distinguish it, while refusing to stipulate an answer in advance.
  1. What would "established" even mean? Three ascending standards of warrant, each with different consequences:
  2. Deductive proof: a valid argument from necessarily true premises. For a contingent being's existence this requires a necessarily true premise plus bridge principles — both contested.
  3. Empirical confirmation: repeated, converging, instrumented observation, as in the sciences.
  4. Abductive inference: the conclusion is the best explanation of data that admit of rivals.

A key asymmetry frames everything below: disconfirmation and confirmation are not symmetric. Many core divine claims are structured so as to be logically consistent with any body of empirical evidence, which blocks decisive disproof; but by that very fact they also fail to issue in unique, risky predictions, which blocks decisive confirmation as well. That asymmetry is the crux of the whole inquiry.

  1. The category problem. The word "proof" is loaded. In mathematics, proof is airtight derivation; in law, proof is "beyond reasonable doubt" before a tribunal; in empirical science "proof" is approximated but never attained — only corroboration or refutation. When we ask for "proof of God," we must first state which standard we are invoking and to what tribunal we are appealing. That question precedes the merits of any particular claim.

Part 1 — Comparative taxonomy of divine conceptions

For each family I state (a) what it asserts about the number, nature, and attributes of the divine; (b) its testable subclaims [T]; (c) its non-testable core [NT]; and (d) what the evidence currently shows [O] — given only where the family has testable subclaims, since a family with none has nothing to report. The consolidating question of what would count as evidence for and against each class of claim is handled once, by class, in Part 4, with a worked per-family example at §1.1 below.

1.1 Classical monotheism (Judaism, Christianity, Islam)

Number / nature / attributes. One God, creator of all, personal/agentive, omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent, and — in classical theism (Augustine, Aquinas, Maimonides, al-Ghazali, Ibn Sina) — necessary, eternal, and in some formulations "beyond being" (Aquinas's ipsum esse subsistens; the God of Exodus 3:14, "I am who I am"). The three traditions differ chiefly on revelation and incarnation: Judaism rejects the Trinity and the messiahship of Jesus; Christianity affirms Trinity and Incarnation; Islam affirms absolute oneness (tawḥīd), rejecting any divine sonship.

Testable subclaims [T]:

  • [T] Foundational historical events. The Exodus and conquest (Judaism); the crucifixion under Pontius Pilate and the reported resurrection appearances (Christianity); the Qur'an's accounts of biblical figures and of its historical context (Islam).
  • [T] Revelation and textual claims. That scripture is historically accurate in its assertions about the world (chronology, geography, quoted prophecies, historical claims).
  • [T] Reported miracles. Verifiable, law-violating events (the Lourdes material below is the worked example).

Non-testable core [NT]:

  • [NT] "God is the ground of being / ipsum esse / beyond being." Stated in negative terms ("beyond," "ineffable"), it predicts nothing the empirical world could contradict.
  • [NT] "God is omnibenevolent; the apparent order of the world is ultimately good/just." Compatible with any observed distribution of good and evil via auxiliary claims (greater good, free will, eschatological compensation, inscrutable reasons).
  • [NT] "God is a necessarily existing being." A logical/metaphysical claim, not an empirical one.

Evidence structure [E+ / E−]:

  • [E+] A foundation event would be corroborated by contemporaneous independent material: e.g., for the Exodus, Egyptian records or artifacts of a large Semitic group departing c. 13th c. BCE, a matching Sinai assemblage, and conquest destruction layers distinct from indigenous change.
  • [E−] Positive disconfirmation of that event (e.g., demonstrated impossibility), or an internal contradiction that cannot be reconciled by any legitimate reading.

What the evidence shows [O]:

  • Exodus/conquest: The mainstream archaeological position (Finkelstein & Silberman, The Bible Unearthed, 2001; Dever, Who Were the Early Israelites…, 2003) is that there is no corroborating evidence for a mass Exodus from Egypt or a foreign conquest, and that Iron-I Israelite settlement of the highlands is best explained as largely indigenous Canaanite development (a settlement emergence, then an Israelite ethnogenesis). Absence of expected evidence is not evidence of impossibility; some specialists (e.g. Hoffmeier's Egyptological arguments, Israel in Egypt, 1996; Kitchen on wider ANE reliability) argue for a smaller historical kernel. Net [O]: the specific large-scale narrative as written is not currently corroborated; the existence of a reduced migration remains an open, minority-debated question. Note the asymmetry — there is no positive refutation either.
  • Crucifixion / resurrection: The crucifixion of Jesus of Nazareth under Pilate is treated as near-certain by mainstream historical scholarship (multiple, independent attestation; e.g. Meier, A Marginal Jew, 1991). The resurrection is not treated as a settled historical fact; scholarly opinion ranges from those who argue for historically defensible appearances/an empty tomb (Habermas, Licona, Wright) to those who hold that historians cannot establish miracle claims at all (Lüdemann; and famously Ehrman, How Jesus Became God, 2014). Net [O]: a widely accepted historical core exists (Jesus, his baptism, his crucifixion); the resurrection remains genuinely contested rather than established by consensus. The earliest texts themselves signal the point — "we are witnesses of these things" (Acts 5:32) — i.e., the claim rests on testimony, which is precisely what does not amount to empirical proof.
  • Modern miracle claims (Lourdes): The Lourdes International Medical Committee has, since the late 19th century, evaluated reported cures by criteria requiring sudden, immediate, complete, and lasting recovery without an established medical explanation; the Catholic Church has recognized on the order of 70 cures as miraculous to date (the most recent widely publicized case recognized in 2018). These are documented medical remissions [T/O] — real events. The inference from "medically unexplained" to "supernatural" is the contested non-empirical step, since competing natural explanations remain (spontaneous remission, misdiagnosis, statistical base rates, psychosomatic factors, and the so-called "Lourdes effect" — a lower-than-background rate of reported cures, which complicates a straightforwardly miraculous reading). Net [O]: unexplained remissions are real data; they do not compel the supernatural interpretation, and the Lourdes effect actively complicates it.

1.2 Polytheism (ancient Near Eastern, Greco-Roman, and living polytheisms)

Number / nature / attributes. Many gods, each limited in power and knowledge, domain-specific (sky, sea, war, fertility), personal and anthropomorphic, often neither omnipotent nor omniscient, and themselves subject to fate or older principles.

Testable subclaims [T]:

  • [T] Historical claims about what was believed and practiced. Readily testable via inscriptions, tablets, temples, and liturgies, which confirm that these beings were worshipped with specified attributes.
  • [T] Specific causal/climatological claims embedded in myth and ritual (e.g., that a storm-god's action governs rainfall).

Non-testable core [NT]:

  • [NT] The existence of the gods themselves, when defined as non-empirical agencies. Note, however, that polytheistic claims are often more testable in principle than classical theism, because they issue in more concrete, contactable predictions (a god who visibly acts and mentors heroes) than a God defined as "beyond being."

What the evidence shows [O]: The beliefs and practices are archaeologically and textually well attested [O] — the primary evidence is direct: Akkadian/Sumerian and Hittite mythological tablets (notably the Enūma eliš), the Ugaritic Baal cycle from Ras Shamra, Greek literary sources (Hesiod's Theogony, Homer, the Homeric Hymns), and the temples, votives, and liturgies themselves. The specific causal claims of myth-religious cosmology are not supported by modern science (e.g., the cosmological roles assigned to particular gods — a storm-god governing rainfall, a divine body whose dismemberment forms the world) [O]; this is the ordinary result of physical explanation displacing mythological etiology, not a special argument against polytheism. Net [O]: the broad structure of polytheistic claims admits tests in a way "beyond being" claims do not, and the evidence does not support their literal cosmological-causal claims.

1.3 Hindu traditions — the devotional/polytheistic strand and the non-dualist strand

An essential clarification: "Hinduism" is not one position, so it appears under two tags the brief names:

  • **(a) Devotional (popular and bhakti Śaivite/Vaiṣṇava), often treated as "polytheism":** many devatās are worshipped, but frequently as forms or aspects of one ultimate reality (henotheistic). Attributes: the gods are personal and powerful, but (outside some accounts of an ultimate Īśvara) are not the single creator-sovereign of the Abrahamic type.
  • (b) Advaita Vedānta (Śaṅkara): non-dualist. Brahman is the sole reality, non-personal, nirguṇa (without attributes), beyond being/non-being and beyond the subject–object distinction; empirical plurality is māyā. Per the brief's ground truth: it does not posit a personal creator god in the Abrahamic sense. Saguṇa Brahman (Īśvara) is a provisional/conventional (vyāvahārika) truth, sublated at the highest (pāramārthika) standpoint, where Ātman = Brahman.

Testable [T] — both strands share the following:

  • [T] Epic and Purāṇic narratives as history: named persons, battles, lineages, and dates (e.g. the Kurukṣetra war, placed by tradition at 3102 BCE, the opening of the Kali Yuga).
  • [T] The classical Purāṇic cosmology: Mount Meru at the centre, the concentric island-continents (dvīpas), a flat earth-disc, the animal supports.
  • [T] Sectarian historical claims: the founders, dates, and spread of particular sampradāyas.

Non-testable core [NT]:

  • [NT] Devotional strand: that the devatās are personal agents who hear, accept, and respond to worship, and that bhakti toward them is efficacious. [NT] Advaita strand: "Brahman is beyond being and cannot be captured by concepts" — by construction untestable; soteriological claims (liberation through knowledge, jīvanmukti) bear on the tradition's own practice, not on external empirical prediction.

What the evidence shows [O]: The epic narratives (Mahābhārata, Rāmāyaṇa) and their specific events/dates are not independently corroborated archaeologically — no destruction layer, inscription, or synchronism matches a Kurukṣetra-scale war at the traditional date [O]. The classical Purāṇic cosmology is not supported by modern cosmology/astronomy [O]. Net [O]: the traditional chronology lacks independent corroboration, though this is a claim about the epics' historicity, not about the metaphysical position of either strand.

1.4 Pantheism, Panentheism

  • **Pantheism (Spinoza; Deus sive Natura):** God is identical to the universe/nature as a totality; there is no personal god, no separate creator, and no miracles (nature follows necessarily from the divine essence).
  • [T] Its empirical commitments: (i) there are no miracles — no events deviating from the order of nature; (ii) everything follows necessarily from the divine nature, i.e. universal determinism; (iii) there is no providential particularity — no special regard for any creature. Each is a checkable claim about how the world behaves.
  • [NT] The identity claim itself — that the totality so ordered is God. No observation distinguishes "the universe is divine" from "the universe simply is," because the two readings license identical predictions. This is the general reason an identity-with-the-totality thesis is untestable.
  • [O] (i) is supported in the negative sense that no physical-law violation is accepted by physics — but this is weak support, since a claim that predicts nothing positive is trivially easy to be consistent with. (ii) is not straightforwardly supported: bare quantum indeterminacy, on standard interpretations, sits in tension with universal determinism, and current physics does not settle the matter. So one of pantheism's checkable commitments may be in tension with part of modern physics, while the others are too negative to be strongly confirmed.
  • Panentheism (process theology — Whitehead, Hartshorne; panentheistic readings in some Christianity and Vedānta; Tillich's "ground of being"): the world is in God, but God is more than the world; God is dipolar (affected by and affecting the world), and often not omnipotent in the classical sense.
  • [T] Its distinguishing commitments, where they claim specific effects: divine action operates by persuasion rather than coercion within natural processes, so no exception to natural law should ever be found; and (in process forms) God's power is genuinely limited. These are checkable, and checkable against classical theism's blanket "God can do anything."
  • [NT] That the world is "in" God and that God is affected by the world in the metaphysical sense. Nothing in the stream of events distinguishes "God is dipolar" from "the world simply changes," absent an independent identification of the divine relatum — which is precisely what is in question. Panentheism's testable side is thus mostly its denial of classical omnipotence, not its positive thesis.
  • [O] Process panentheism's checkable commitments are negative (no coercive miracles), and the evidence is consistent with them in the same weak sense as pantheism's: physics recognizes no accepted law-violating interruption. As a claim about why no such interruption occurs — persuasion vs. non-intervention vs. physical closure — the underdetermination is total, so the [O] evidence does not discriminate among the three.

1.5 Deism (clock-maker design; modern design/fine-tuning cosmology)

Number / nature / attributes. One creator-God who designed or fine-tuned the universe and then does not intervene (no revelation, no miracles); commonly inferred from apparent design or cosmic order.

Testable subclaims [T]:

  • [T] Fine-tuning / design in physical constants and cosmic conditions. The deist/design claim with the most genuine empirical content.
  • [T] Non-intervention. Deism predicts no miracles and no revelation — a checkable negative prediction, though in practice indistinguishable from pantheism's and panentheism's no-coercion commitments (§1.4), which is itself a reason it does not discriminate.

Non-testable core [NT]:

  • [NT] That the designer is a moral/personal agent identical to any particular traditional God.

What the evidence shows [O]: The universe's conditions do appear, on widely used tabulations, to be constrained to a narrow range compatible with life — the values of certain physical constants (the cosmological constant; the structure of nuclear and electromagnetic forces) — as catalogued in Barrow & Tipler, The Anthropic Cosmological Principle (1986). (Note that "fine-tuning" is itself a contested label: which constants vary, and how improbably their values are set, are live questions, flagged here rather than assumed.) for the design-side development of the argument, Robin Collins, "The Teleological Argument" (in Craig & Moreland, eds., The Blackwell Companion to Natural Theology, 2009). But the inference is underdetermined: the same observations are explained without design by observer-selection effects (the weak/strong anthropic principle), multiverse hypotheses, or brute necessity; Weinberg's work on the cosmological constant is a standard skeptical reference point. Net [O]: the fine-tuning data are among the strongest empirical data bearing on any divine claim, yet they admit of multiple live explanations and so are not decisive. Note further that fine-tuning speaks, at most, to design — not to the designer's nature (benevolence, personality, number) and not to which tradition's doctrine is correct.

1.6 Non-theistic traditions: Advaita Vedānta (see 1.3b), Buddhism, Jainism

  • Buddhism (esp. its early metaphysics): Anattā (no permanent self), dukkha, anicca. The tradition is non-theistic rather than strictly "atheistic": its teachings neither require nor center a creator God, and its metaphysics sits awkwardly with a creator-sovereign of the Abrahamic type. Some Mahāyāna developments (Buddha-nature; the Ādi-Buddha framing in certain Vajrayāna lines) reintroduce an ultimate reality that functions analogously to "God" — itself a contested internal matter.
  • [T] Buddhist cosmology (Mount Meru and the flat-earth world-system beneath it, the layout of the realms) — not supported by modern cosmology/astronomy [O].
  • [T] Historical claims about the Buddha's life — broadly attested within the tradition; the texts are internally consistent but not independently confirmed by inscription or material record. Specific dates remain historically debated (the "long chronology" vs. "short chronology" dispute over the Aśokan interval; cf. Bechert, ed., Die Datierung des historischen Buddha / Symposium on the Dating of the Historical Buddha, 4 parts, 1991–97, and therein Richard Gombrich, "Dating the Buddha: A Red Herring Revealed"; for the wider social history, Gombrich, Theravāda Buddhism: A Social History from Ancient Benares to Modern Colombo, 1988).
  • [T] Claims about the tradition's extent — these are empirically robust: the Aśokan edicts (Major Rock Edicts; the Lumbini pillar inscription naming the Buddha's birthplace and the title Buddha Śākya-muni) are real archaeological artifacts [O] (epigraphic treatment in Romila Thapar, Aśoka and the Decline of the Mauryas, 1961/1997 — a scholarly monograph, not a text edition; for editions of the inscriptions themselves see Hultzsch, Inscriptions of Asoka, 1925, and Bloch, Les Inscriptions d'Asoka, 1950), and the historical spread of Buddhism across Asia is documented. These are facts about the tradition, not about its metaphysics.
  • [NT] Rebirth, karma operating across lives, nirvāṇa — not empirically testable.
  • Jainism: explicitly teaches no creator god; reality comprises souls (jīva) and matter; liberation (mokṣa) comes by the "three jewels." Like Buddhism it asserts 0 rather than "one in some sense"; the difference is that for Jainism this is an explicit doctrinal thesis (there is no creator, and omniscience belongs to liberated souls, not to a maker), whereas Buddhism is non-theistic more by omission — it simply has no role for a creator-sovereign.
  • [T] Historical claims about Mahāvīra as a contemporary of the Buddha, and about the two sects (Śvetāmbara / Digambara); textual-historical claims about the canon (the Āgamas and their redaction; the Mathurā inscriptions). [O] The broad historical framework — a 6th–5th c. BCE śramaṇa milieu alongside Buddhism — is well supported; the internal dating of the canon and the details of the sectarian split remain scholarly reconstruction (Dundas, The Jains, 1992/2002). [NT] Liberation, karma conceived as subtle matter (karma-pudgala) adhering to the soul, and the Jain cosmic structure (three worlds, the loka-ākāśa).

Part 3 — The logical structure of the problem

3.1 The argument from religious diversity

Reconstructed (Hick, An Interpretation of Religion, 1989): religious belief is strongly correlated with birth-culture (people overwhelmingly adopt the religion of their upbringing); the great traditions make mutually incompatible truth-claims; if exactly one were uniquely true, the tight belief/culture correlation would be puzzling; so claims to unique truth are undercut. At bottom it attacks the epistemic reliability of the mechanisms that generate exclusive belief. Standard responses: (i) culture-transmission is how truth is also passed on (a real point, but one the exclusivist must earn); (ii) pluralism is itself a further hypothesis that requires argument. Decidable? It is empirical as to the correlation and the incompatibility [T/O], but its force against exclusivism is philosophical, not deductively decisive.

3.2 The problem of divine hiddenness (Schellenberg)

Argument (Divine Hiddenness and Human Reason, 1993): if a perfectly loving God exists, then no one who is not culpably resistant would remain in reasonable nonbelief; but honest, truth-seeking, non-resistant nonbelievers exist; so [the argument concludes] there is no perfectly loving God. (The bracketed conclusion is reconstructed as part of the argument; it is reported here, not endorsed — and see the "Decidable?" note below.)

Structure: a modus ponens with an empirical minor premise (that non-resistant nonbelievers exist) and a conceptual major premise (a perfectly loving God would prevent non-resistant nonbelief). The minor premise is checkable in principle [T]; the major premise can only be won if one's conception of "perfectly loving," and of what such a God would do, prevails. Standard responses: a greater-good / soul-making defense (hiddenness enables freely-grown relationship — a free-will-adjacent move); and "God may have reasons we cannot see," which renders the premise compatible with any data. Decidable? The minor premise is observable; the major is normative/conceptual. The argument is strong but not deductively coercing, because defenders can hold a conception of divine love on which hiddenness is compatible with love, or invoke epistemic humility about God's reasons — precisely the "consistent with any evidence" escape hatch that blocks decisive disproof.

3.3 The problem of evil

  • Logical problem: the set {(1) omnipotent, (2) omnibenevolent, (3) omniscient God; (4) evil exists} is inconsistent; Mackie ("Evil and Omnipotence," Mind, 1955) took the implication to be that the existence of such a God is ruled out. The majority position since Plantinga (God, Freedom, and Evil, 1974) — though a contested one, especially for natural evil, where transworld-depravity-style premises are harder to sustain — is that the deductive form fails: a free-will defense exhibits a logically possible world in which (1)–(3) and (4) cohere (creatures with significant freedom can bring about evil, and a world with freedom is better), so the stated inconsistency is not established.
  • Evidential problem (Rowe): on this line of argument, the quantity, kinds, and distribution of apparently pointless suffering (e.g., intense suffering of the apparently innocent, including animals) is offered as evidence that lowers the probability of such a God, even if it does not deductively refute it. This is reported as a position in the literature, not adopted here as a finding; it is a Bayesian (probabilistic) claim that depends heavily on prior credences, so it is not decisive in either direction by construction (cf. §6.2 note 2 and §6.4: this document deliberately assigns no numerical value to the evil or hiddenness cases, in either direction, because the auxiliaries that would flatten them are themselves contested).

Decidable? The logical form is largely answered by the free-will defense; the evidential form is genuinely probative for its proponents but probabilistic and prior-dependent — so even on its own terms it weighs only within a Bayesian framework whose priors are exactly what is in dispute, and it cannot deliver a verdict.

3.4 The Euthyphro dilemma (Plato, Euthyphro, ~10a)

As recorded in Plato, the question is: is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods? Translated into the modern dilemma:

  • Horn 1 — independence: what is good is independent of God (then God conforms to an external moral standard, which limits divine sovereignty/omnipotence).
  • Horn 2 — arbitrariness: what is good is good only because God commands it (then morality is arbitrary — God could have commanded cruelty — which many find unacceptable and which undercuts calling God good in a non-vacuous way).

There is a well-known third move, the standard theistic reply: a perfect-being / essentialist response — God is the good, or the divine perfect nature constitutes the standard, so goodness is neither external nor arbitrary but grounded in that nature (cf. Aquinas on divine goodness; later, Robert M. Adams, Finite and Infinite Goods, 1999). Critics answer that this is either a restatement of the dilemma in disguise or a stipulation that itself needs defending. The dilemma is widely taken to be genuine, which is why theistic ethicologists have worked so hard on the divine-nature response; whether it is instead a false dilemma is itself contested, and the underlying realism question is not settled by anyone. Decidable? This is a metaethical/logical dilemma, not an empirical test. Its force depends on unresolved background commitments about moral realism and divine sovereignty.

3.5 Why "proof" is the wrong category

Several independent reasons, each sufficient on its own:

  1. Falsifiability (Popper): a claim repeatedly modified to accommodate any possible observation is, by definition, unfalsifiable — and unfalsifiable claims are exactly the sort proof cannot attach to. Divine claims in their strong metaphysical forms (beyond being, ground of consciousness, necessarily existing, inscrutable) are, by their own terms, fitted to every possible world.
  2. Underdetermination (Duhem–Quine): data never map one-to-one onto hypotheses; auxiliary hypotheses always mediate. A "miracle" underdetermines between "supernatural cause" and "unknown natural cause"; fine-tuning underdetermines between "designer" and "multiverse / selection / necessity." Underdetermination blocks the uniqueness that proof would require.
  3. Evidential asymmetry: hiddenness, absence of evidence, and the problem of evil pull one way, but any tradition can always invoke inscrutable reasons to keep its core claim compatible with the evidence — the very move that prevents disproof also prevents proof.
  4. Circularity: if you define God as that which necessarily exists, the question is answered by stipulation; if you define God impersonally enough to escape the circularity, you deflate the claim to fit every observation, again blocking proof. The circle closes in both directions.
  5. Category of evidence: "proof" for an empirical claim needs instruments and records; "proof" for a mathematical claim needs derivation. The core divine claims sit in neither category — they are framework/interpretive commitments under which empirical data are read.

Part 6 — Quantifying the bound: how much could any evidence ever do?

Parts 2–5 argued qualitatively that the testable subclaims sit at the periphery and the core resists adjudication. This section makes that bound numerical. The point of the arithmetic is not to assign a probability to any god — that would be a verdict, and none is attempted here. The point is to show how little any body of evidence could move the question, even if the evidence were far better than anything currently available. All figures below are plain Bayes arithmetic and were computed directly; they are reproducible from the formulas given.

6.1 Existence and identification are different problems, and the gap is ~4 orders of magnitude

"Is there a true God?" bundles two questions with very different evidential budgets:

  • Existence: is some conception from Part 1 actual?
  • Identification: which one is it?

Suppose evidence arrived that did everything it possibly could for existence — raising "some divine reality exists" to a posterior of 0.999 (a Bayes factor of ~1000:1 against "none," which is far stronger than anything in the ledger; the strongest entry, fine-tuning, is underdetermined and carries a BF nowhere near this). The identification question would remain entirely open, because such evidence does not discriminate among candidates. Worked example (fine-tuning, ledger row 7). A conclusion that is determined by the prior rather than by the data is, by definition, not an evidential conclusion. 7.2 Why Layer-A refutation does not localize to the core

Let C be a tradition's core commitment and A₁…Aₙ the auxiliaries needed to derive a dated prediction P from it — a hermeneutic principle, a calendar or arithmetic computation, and a claim about how literally the prediction is to be read. Then:

  • P is entailed by (C ∧ A₁ ∧ … ∧ Aₙ). Observing ¬P licenses only ¬C ∨ ¬A₁ ∨ … ∨ ¬Aₙ — a disjunction with n + 1 disjuncts, of which C is one.
  • The observation therefore does not localize: it identifies a failed conjunction, not a failed conjunct. This is a claim about identification, not magnitude. How far ¬P should lower C depends on the independent warrant of the Aᵢ — formally P(C|¬P) = P(C)·[1 − P(A₁…Aₙ|C)]/P(¬P) — a quantity this section does not price, and one the literature disputes: Rowe (1979) and Draper (1989) hold that unpriced catch-all auxiliaries carry a Bayesian cost rather than neutralizing the evidence (§6.2 note 2). Selecting ¬C requires an independent argument that no ¬Aᵢ is available; the record in §7.3 shows that in each case a ¬Aᵢ was in fact selected. The burden is symmetric: the same demand for an independent argument applies to choosing any ¬Aᵢ as to choosing ¬C, and neither choice is warranted by this section.
  • Confirmation is not deductively decisive either: even if P were observed, P does not entail C, since many cores other than C are compatible with P. Whether P then favours C over rivals is a likelihood question — how many rivals also predict P, and at what prior for the auxiliaries — which the entailment structure alone does not settle (cf. §6.4 and §6.2 note 3).

Scope, stated against this document's own caveat. The insulation result above is established for the claim-species actually instantiated in §7.3. As with §6.2, it is not asserted here for personal/agentive or designing core claims generally: those issue more specific expectations, and §6.4 records the strongest of them — religious experience — as out of scope. The dated-event prediction is one subtype, and the result is about that subtype only.

This is the cleanest available account of why the layer at which refutation is easiest is logically insulated from the layer that carries the metaphysical content, for the claim-species examined. A date can be refuted; a thesis about the nature of the divine is not a dated claim and does not become one.

7.4 What this record establishes — and what it does not

Established (claims about the structure of the claims, not about the truth of any religion):

  1. There is a class of religious claim that is maximally falsifiable — the dated event prediction — and in the three documented cases examined every day-level declaration was disconfirmed: 5 of 5.
  2. In those same cases, refutation did not localize to the tradition's core metaphysical commitment (0 of 6 predictions, 0 of 3 traditions). The mechanism is the auxiliary move, which was used in each case; whether a given auxiliary was available on independent grounds before the failure is a different question, not answered here, since the sanctuary doctrine and the "spiritual judgment" were both constructed afterwards.
  3. Non-retraction after a disconfirmed prediction occurs outside religion too, which is what the two secular controls establish — nothing more. In the Simon–Ehrlich wager (1980), Ehrlich predicted rising inflation-adjusted prices in five metals he chose over 1980–1990; prices fell; Ehrlich paid the bet in 1990 while disputing its significance (Paul Sabin, The Bet: Paul Ehrlich, Julian Simon, and Our Gamble over Earth's Future, Yale UP, 2013). And Philip E. Tetlock's Expert Political Judgment (Princeton UP, 2005) found forecast accuracy in the 1988–2003 tournaments to be poor, with single-framework reasoners ("hedgehogs") outperformed by eclectic ones ("foxes"); the belief-updating findings come from separate experimental work. What the controls do not establish: that retraction was warranted in either the religious or the secular cases. In Ehrlich's case, for instance, the auxiliary — that a five-metal price wager does not test the population/environment thesis — is widely regarded as justified, which is exactly why these controls control for behaviour and not for rationality.

Not established — and explicitly disclaimed:

  • Nothing here bears on whether any god exists. Every falsified claim in §7.3 was a dated event prediction, not a thesis about the existence or nature of the divine. A predictor can be wrong about a date while the theological framework is unaffected — and unaffected is not the same as false.
  • The failed tests may simply have been bad tests — if the hermeneutic or the calendar computation was mistaken, abandoning the framework was never warranted by the prediction's failure. The record shows the auxiliary move was used; it does not show the auxiliary move was unjustified. Telling those apart requires an independent argument in each case, which this document does not supply. Correspondingly, no normative label is attached to the pattern: whether non-retraction is a rational failure is not adjudicated here, and the phrase "failure mode" is deliberately avoided.
  • Direction of coverage. This record addresses the disconfirmation direction (a refuted prediction does not localize to the core). It does not address the confirmation direction — whether several purportedly independent successes could accumulate — which is precisely the Swinburne / McGrew & McGrew / Collins cumulative-case strategy, reported in §6.2 note 3 and not adjudicated here.
  • Species of claim. The insulation result is shown for dated-event predictions. It is not asserted for personal/agentive or designing core claims generally (§7.2), and religious experience — the personal-god prediction this document does not examine — remains out of scope (§6.4).
  • Sample. Three well-known traditions are not a random sample. No rate of belief abandonment, no cross-cultural generalization, and no claim about unspecified other movements may be read off this table.

7.5 What would refute Part 7 (stated in advance)

So that this section is itself falsifiable rather than merely an argument:

  • a documented case in which a tradition's dated, specific prediction was falsified and the tradition abandoned its core metaphysical commitment on that basis;
  • evidence that any of the five non-occurrences above did not in fact occur — i.e. that a predicted event did happen;
  • a derivation of a dated prediction from a core commitment without auxiliaries, which would make ¬P localize to ¬C and restore the propagation of refutation.

Part 9 — A formal separation result (companion artifact: god-religions-separation-formal.md, verified by verify_v9.cjs)

Parts 2–8 established the testable/non-testable split row by row and argument by argument. Part 9 converts the load-bearing claim into three computed results, with the script and its first-run audit trail published alongside. Summary only; the full derivation, the regime definitions, the pre-stated refuters, and the limitations are in the companion file.

  • C1 — Pairwise core separability: 0 of 28. Across all 8 families there are 28 unordered pairs. A pair is separable iff some observation is entailed by one family's core and forbidden by the other's. Under the tags this document itself uses (Part 2), no pair is separable. Granting the interventionist traditions the strongest testable reading anyone has demanded of them — that their cores do entail that law-violating interruptions occur — raises the count to 9 of 28 (3 interventionist families × 3 non-interventionist families), or 6 of 28 if classical theism is read strictly. In every regime, all separable pairs reduce to a single observation type: whether a law-violating interruption of natural order occurs. No observation type was found that separates any pair on the question the brief asks — what the divine is. Regime A's count is a consequence of this document's own [NT] tagging and is reported as such: it is the conservative answer to the objection, and Regime B is the upper bound.
  • C2 — The discrimination threshold: 89,991. With a uniform prior over N ≈ 10⁴ candidates, a single observation with likelihood ratio b per rival gives the winner b/(b + N − 1). A 90% posterior on one tradition requires b = 89,991; 50% requires 9,999; 99% requires 989,901. The binary "is there any god?" question at the same 90% target requires only b = 9 — so identification is ~10⁴ times harder than existence, not marginally harder. The ledger's strongest row (fine-tuning, ≈ 3) falls short of the identification threshold by ≈ 30,000×, and the reason is C1: a ratio of 3 is not a weak 89,991, it is a ratio between hypotheses the evidence does not discriminate. Strengthening b without specificity saturates at 0.0100% for any single tradition (§6.1), because the bottleneck past b ≈ 10³ is rival exclusion, not evidence strength.
  • C3 — Duhem–Quine neutrality: the likelihood ratio is exactly 1. A dated prediction is entailed by (core ∧ auxiliaries). Observing its negation has probability 1 under every fault hypothesis — "the core was wrong" and "auxiliary i was wrong" are equally expected — so refutation is evidentially silent about where the fault lies, verified by exhaustive enumeration over the whole assignment space for k = 1…6 auxiliaries. Consequence: the core's share of the fault is whatever the attribution prior says, and three defensible conventions give 0.25 (uniform over fault sites), 0.5333 (uniform over worlds), and 0.625 (core weighted 5× more secure) at k = 3 — a 2.5× spread driven entirely by the prior, with no contribution from the evidence. This is why §7.2 correctly declines to price the residual effect on the core, and why "¬P lowers the core's credence by X" cannot be stated without smuggling in the very prior at issue.

What this section does not do. It assigns no probability to any god's existence and asserts no verdict on any religion. C1's Regime A count inherits this document's own tagging; C2's thresholds scale with a contested N; C3's conventions are a demonstration of prior-dependence, not a defence of one. The companion file states the refuters for each result in advance.

Part 10 — The insulation result: a second companion artifact (god-religions-insulation-formal.md, verified by verify_v10.cjs)

Part 9 computed whether any observation separates the families; separation-formal C3 computed that a refuted dated prediction is evidentially silent about where the fault lies (likelihood ratio exactly 1.0). Part 10 adds the one gate neither computed: whether a refuted dated prediction even reaches the core before attribution begins. The computation: a prediction is entailed by (core ∧ auxiliaries), and if k auxiliary-move families are each tenable under stress with probability a, independently, then the probability that a refutation makes core contact is κ(k; a) = (1 − a)^k — 0.25 at k = 2, a = 0.5, and 0.0625 at the k = 4 families Part 7 documents, with κ ≤ 0.25 for every a ≥ 0.5, k ≥ 2. Chained to C3's gate-2 likelihood ratio of exactly 1.0, the maximally testable layer is shown to deliver zero observation-driven content to the core at both gates: contact is bounded by a prior quantity, and localization is closed identically for every input. The one observational result: Part 7's 0-of-6 record is consistent with auxiliary tenability down to a ≈ 0.41 at the 5% level — so the record does not show that auxiliaries are highly tenable, only that even marginally tenable ones, at the documented k values, insulate the core in a sample of that size. Full tables, per-case grounding, the N-sensitivity lattice for C2, and the pre-stated falsifiers are in the companion file.
